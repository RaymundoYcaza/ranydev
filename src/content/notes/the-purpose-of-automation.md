# What problem should automation solve?

In my other job, as a business process automation specialist, I've been revisiting the question of what automation is really for.

When we automate, why do we do it, and what are we trying to accomplish?

We want to move faster. We want to save time. We want more time. But what for?

## Saving time isn't enough of a purpose

These days, everyone wants to move fast. The question is: where are we rushing to?

Everyone wants to free up time. Everyone wants more time.

But very few people actually stop to figure out what they want that time for. And what they end up doing with it is simply doing more things.

More! Do more!

We don't talk about being results-oriented. We don't even mention having goals.

These days, our purpose is reduced to checking tasks off a checklist, without being entirely clear on what they're for.

### What do we do with the time we free up?

We usually think about automating for efficiency.

And that's not necessarily a bad thing.

But are we sure we have the right definition of efficiency?

I don't just mean the textbook definition. I mean the right definition of efficiency for your specific use case, your business, your context.

Are we talking about efficiency in terms of processing time?

Or maybe efficiency in terms of fixed or variable costs.

Or maybe we want to improve an efficiency metric related to the quality of our product per batch.

What is efficiency, and what exactly are we trying to be efficient at?

Automation shouldn't just serve to increase that frantic efficiency we're all chasing. Or at least, we shouldn't chase efficiency in terms of time alone.

Automation has been sold as a way to improve people's lives, and that's what it should do.

What other purpose could all these technological and methodological advances have, if not to improve people's quality of life, when all is said and done?

My approach has always been to automate so we can free up time wasted on low-value tasks, giving us a chance to focus on what we do best and, in turn, make a powerful, meaningful impact on the quality of our products and people's lives.

Who really benefits from the time saved? A lot of the time, I'm not sure.

## Automate tasks, not people away

### What parts of the work should we hand over to machines?

Let's be clear: a machine will never be responsible for its actions, whether that's failures, bad decisions, or omissions.

Yes, I'm talking mainly about artificial intelligence here.

Yesterday, you wouldn't hold a photovoltaic sensor responsible. You wouldn't hold a piece of software responsible for automatically measuring how much rain fell in a field, either.

So why are so many people now trying to hand responsibility over to AI in processes that need human involvement, like certain customer service tasks?

Generative models are probabilistic tools.

You already know that.

What you might not know, or might have stopped paying attention to, is how high an error rate these tools can have because of their probabilistic nature.

Repetitive tasks within a tightly defined process are the best candidates for automation. But just because a task is repetitive doesn't mean it's completely predictable or requires no human judgment.

Even a complex task that clearly needs a person to do it can be broken down into subtasks and further levels, until we find the microtasks that could be automated and separate them from the ones that require human judgment.

That's the point: keep people involved, use human talent effectively, and focus them on high-value tasks, both for the business and for their own sense of purpose.

We've built society on interdependence, relying on other people's talents to reach our goals. Technology has helped us protect our health and our lives, and even take unpleasant tasks off our hands. It's also helped us stay connected with others and create remote teams capable of taking on big projects.

So why should technology now be used to get rid of people, eliminate dependencies, and cut connections?

Let's ask ourselves what we lose when an interaction or an occupation is fully automated and the person behind it is discarded.

# Who benefits, and who gets left behind?

Beyond asking what we can gain from automation, we need to look at how those benefits are distributed and who ends up paying the price.

And I'm not talking specifically about how money is distributed. I also mean benefits like a lighter workload and reduced psychosocial risks.

It's possible that only the business owner sees any financial benefit, while operators don't get a lighter workload at all. Instead, their workload may actually grow. This increases resistance to change in organizations, especially when the changes are driven by technology and automation.

## Benefits don't distribute themselves

A productivity gain isn't always the answer. Other factors matter when determining whether there's a real benefit.

Yes, I'm saying that increased productivity doesn't always benefit the business.

And if we add to that the fact that employees don't necessarily benefit from automation, then we're looking at an illusion of opportunity, not a real one.

So a productivity gain is in no way, by itself, a guarantee of improvement for everyone involved.

We need to think in terms of business intelligence, process analysis, methods engineering, curve analysis, and so on before making a strategic decision that looks only at automation.

### Who gains time, money, or power?

Automating processes can benefit the company, sure. But it should also improve the lives of the people doing the work and the people receiving the service or product.

If all we end up doing is concentrating the benefits, whether that's savings or greater capacity, then maybe we're calling something progress when it only benefits a few.

## The cost of automation

When we try to sell technology or automation, we tend to talk only about the benefits. But the benefits aren't the whole story.

We also need to consider what's lost, who bears the consequences, and whether all these costs are being taken into account.

### What happens to people who depend on the tasks being replaced?

These days, automating tasks seems easy, at least on the surface.

But when a task disappears, the needs of the person who used to do it don't magically disappear with it.

The question isn't just whether someone will lose their job.

What happens to the skills and experience that person developed?

Will they have a chance to take on new responsibilities or contribute to other parts of the process?

Sometimes, the task being automated doesn't eliminate a position. It changes the work of the people who are still there.

And here's the interesting part.

That change doesn't always mean less work. The time saved can turn into more low-value tasks, more pressure, or an expectation to adapt without support.

That's why the cost of automation should also include how that transition is handled. If the company gets the benefit, it doesn't seem reasonable to leave each worker to deal on their own with lost responsibilities, training, or the consequences of a system failure.

And when something goes wrong, responsibility still belongs to the people and organizations that chose to implement the automation. A machine can perform a task, but it can't take responsibility for what its decision means to the people who depend on it.

# Choosing what we want to automate

If automation isn't the goal in itself, then the next question is what to automate. By that, I mean deciding which tasks are worth handing over and which ones we want to keep under human judgment.

And guess what?

That takes more judgment. High-level judgment.

## Technical capability doesn't decide for us

The fact that a technology can do something only proves it's possible. It doesn't mean it's a good idea, let alone that we should hand it control of the entire process.

Every day, we see news about this or that technology, or this or that AI model, doing more things and doing them faster.

And here we should ask ourselves: do I really need more speed?

Or maybe what I need is more consistency?

Do I need the power of inference, even if the "reasoning" process takes an hour? Or do I just need a deterministic process that gets it done in a few seconds?

What is the right question for your process, and where do you find it?

### If something can be automated, does that mean it should be?

Following that line of thought, the fact that something can be automated doesn't mean it's a good idea.

We can and should choose to keep human involvement in some tasks where context, trust, or responsibility matter, and automate only the parts that actually help.

## A more human purpose

In the end, automation shouldn't be measured only by how much faster it helps us get things done, but by how it helps us do better as people and members of society.

What we should aim for with automation isn't simply to serve faster, but to serve better.

### How can we tell if an automation is worth it?

One way to judge automation is to ask how much pointless work it can reduce without devaluing people or turning them into disposable parts of the system.

In this case, the measure wouldn't be just how much it produces, but what opportunities it opens up and what human abilities it helps us preserve.
